# Benchmark Report: BoundedByteBuffer against the byte-array streams

1. Identification of the codebase
   - **Repository:** `https://github.com/Inqudium/legatium.git`
   - **Commit hash (HEAD at measurement time):** `3232b134acf0f58d0c4f220cc614ceb920f436ce` (full; the merge of PR #14, which reworked the buffer's growth and truncated rendering)
   - **Reference (branch):** `refs/heads/chore/bounded-buffer-benchmarks` (branched from `main` at that commit; the branch adds only the `./benchmarks/` module, the CI smoke job and this report)
2. Question under measurement
   - No PERF_ANALYSIS precedes this report. The question is the design one behind ADR-0003's shared buffer: what `eu.inqudium.legatium.common.BoundedByteBuffer` costs, in time and in bytes allocated per body, against the two streams it could have been built on - the JDK's `java.io.ByteArrayOutputStream` and Spring's `org.springframework.util.FastByteArrayOutputStream` - on the WRITE path (buffering one body) and on the RENDER path (turning it into the logged string).
   - Not measured: the buffer BEFORE PR #14 (the 4N-vs-3N claim of that PR's truncated path is asserted from the JDK's String internals, not re-measured here); the twins' tees around the buffer (the buffer is the unit); the UTF-8 fast path of branch `perf/utf8-truncated-fast-path`, which is not on `main`.
3. Measurement environment
   - **Machine:** AMD Ryzen AI 9 HX PRO 370 w/ Radeon 890M (12 cores / 24 threads), 86 GiB RAM; developer workstation, near-idle; governor `powersave`, **turbo ENABLED** - clock not pinned, so absolute times carry a few percent of noise beyond the reported CIs.
   - **JVM:** Oracle JDK 26.0.1 - **target runtime is Java 21; no JDK 21 installed.** Time figures are ratio evidence; `gc.alloc.rate.norm` is robust across JDKs.
   - **JMH:** 1.37; every result from 3 forks × (5 warmup + 8 measurement) iterations × 1 s, `-prof gc`; GC and heap at JVM defaults.
   - **Comparability to production:** relative differences and allocation counts meaningful; absolute latencies indicative.
4. Placement & output
   - **Benchmark sources:** `./benchmarks/` (new standalone module, no reactor child - see its POM comment; `mvn -DskipTests install` at the root, then `mvn -f benchmarks/pom.xml package`)
   - **Raw output (the evidence):** `./benchmarks/results/body-buffer-write.{txt,json}`, `./benchmarks/results/body-buffer-render.{txt,json}`
   - **Discarded run:** the first write run returned each buffer's SIZE instead of the buffer; the JIT scalar-replaced the non-escaping buffers and dropped the dead stores for some variants (a presized JDK stream "wrote" 1 KiB in 44 ns, the hinted buffer came out 4× slower than the unhinted one on identical code). The benchmark now returns the buffer object to the blackhole; the numbers below are from the second run only. The KDoc of `BodyBufferWriteBenchmark` records the trap.

## 1. Executive summary

On the **bulk write path** (8 KiB chunks, the shape of every bulk read) the three buffers are within noise of each other, time and bytes alike: a 16 KiB body costs about 1.1 µs and 24.6 KB of allocation without a size hint, about 0.7 µs and 16.4 KB with one - the buffer's `expect` behaves exactly like presizing a stream. On the **byte-wise write path** (the `InputStream.read()` shape of a byte-wise reader through the blocking twin's tee) the buffer is the fastest of the three by a clear margin: 16 KiB in 19-24 µs against 40 µs for Spring's stream and 77-140 µs for the JDK's, whose `write(int)` takes a monitor per byte. Two costs the buffer pays by design are visible and as documented: the **64 KiB hint ceiling** makes a truthful 256 KiB declaration allocate 459 KB instead of 262 KB and take 19.7 µs instead of the 10.5 µs of a presized stream (two doublings from the ceiling), and the **discard path** beyond the cap costs 0.3 ns per discarded byte byte-wise and nothing in bulk. On the **render path** the complete rendering equals the streams' `toString(Charset)` to the byte and within 1-2 % in time; the **truncated rendering** on `main` (the generic scratch-buffer loop) allocates the same 2N as the naive `String(bytes) + note` for ASCII (526.7 vs 524.4 KB at 256 KiB) but takes 1.56× its time (37.1 vs 23.8 µs), while for two-byte text it allocates 28 % LESS than the naive concatenation (439 vs 612 KB) at 1.11× its time. The truncated path is the one place a change is worth having: the UTF-8 fast path of `perf/utf8-truncated-fast-path` targets exactly the ASCII gap and would bring it to the naive path's time at the same allocation.

## 2. Instruments

- `BodyBufferWriteBenchmark` - one operation = buffering ONE body of `bodyKb` ∈ {1, 16, 256} into a FRESH buffer, `chunk` ∈ {1 (byte-wise), 8192 (bulk)}. Seven cases: the buffer without a hint, with `expect(body)`, and with the shipped 16 KiB cap against the full body (the discard path); the JDK stream default (32) and presized; Spring's stream default (256-byte first block) and presized. The cap equals the body in the first six, so every buffer keeps every byte and the difference is growth strategy plus the JDK stream's `synchronized`.
- `BodyBufferRenderBenchmark` - one operation = rendering ONE buffered body of `bodyKb` ∈ {16, 256}, `content` ∈ {ascii, "aä" alternating}, filled once in setup. Five cases: the buffer complete (`total == size`) and truncated (`total == size + 1`), both streams' `toString(UTF_8)`, and the naive truncated rendering `new String(bytes, 0, size, UTF_8) + note`.

## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Bulk writes: no cost over the streams - Confirmed

At every size the buffer, the JDK stream and Spring's stream are within 10 % of each other in time and within a few hundred bytes in allocation. The growth strategies are the same in effect (doubling from a floor of 256, 32 and 256 bytes respectively), and the bulk path has no per-call overhead to speak of. `expect(length)` is the exact equivalent of presizing: 0.69 vs 0.67/0.68 µs and 16.4 KB against 16.4/16.6 KB at 16 KiB.

### 4.2 Byte-wise writes: the buffer is the fastest of the three - Confirmed

The buffer writes a byte in about 1.2-1.5 ns (0.8 ns at 256 KiB, where the loop dominates), Spring's stream in 2.2-2.5 ns, the JDK stream in 4.7-8.7 ns: `ByteArrayOutputStream.write(int)` is `synchronized`, and since biased locking left the JDK (15) every call pays an uncontended monitor. Presizing the JDK stream halves its time (no growth checks inside the lock) but keeps the lock. For the blocking twin's tee, whose `read()` forwards byte by byte, the choice of a bare array over `ByteArrayOutputStream` (ADR-0003, the KDoc's "a bare array rather than a `ByteArrayOutputStream`") is thus measured: 6-11× at the byte-wise shape. The 16 KiB unhinted point carries a wide CI (± 5.2 µs); the ordering is unaffected.

### 4.3 The 64 KiB hint ceiling costs a truthful large declaration two doublings - Confirmed, as documented

At 256 KiB with a truthful `Content-Length`, the buffer allocates 458 832 B (65 536 + 131 072 + 262 144) and takes 19.7 µs, where a presized stream allocates 262 184 B in 10.5 µs. That is the documented price of `MAX_HINTED_CAPACITY` (PR #14: a byte of a body declared huge must not reserve a large cap). Caps up to 64 KiB are unaffected - at 16 KiB the hinted buffer equals the presized streams. Whoever sets a cap in the hundreds of KiB pays about 1.75× the cap in transient allocation per body that fills it; the alternative (no ceiling) pays the cap per body that lies about its length. No change recommended; the number is the argument for keeping the default cap at 16 KiB.

### 4.4 Bytes beyond the cap cost nothing in bulk, 0.3 ns each byte-wise - Confirmed

The 16 KiB-capped buffer fed a 256 KiB body allocates 24 640 B and takes 1.08 µs in bulk - exactly the 16 KiB case, the surplus is clipped in the ranged write before any copy. Byte-wise it takes 83.7 µs for 262 144 calls, of which 16 384 store a byte: the remaining 245 760 calls cost the `size < maxBytes` compare each, about 0.3 ns.

### 4.5 Complete rendering equals the streams' `toString` - Confirmed

Identical allocation to the byte (one compact string; for "aä" the JDK's Latin1 attempt plus the trimmed copy) and time within 1-2 %: the buffer's complete path IS `String(bytes, 0, size, charset)`.

### 4.6 Truncated rendering on `main`: same bytes as the naive concatenation for ASCII, 1.56× its time - Confirmed, the one place worth a change

The scratch-buffer loop (PR #14) allocates the builder and the result - 526 696 B at 256 KiB ASCII - where the naive `String(bytes) + note` allocates the prefix string and the concatenation - 524 384 B. Same footprint; but the generic `CharsetDecoder` loop and the per-chunk `StringBuilder.append(char[])` take 37.1 µs against the naive path's 23.8 µs through the JDK's UTF-8 intrinsics. For two-byte text the picture inverts on bytes: 439 313 B against 611 817 B (the builder takes the chars straight from the decoder, the naive path copies the prefix string once more into the concatenation) at 182 vs 164 µs. Verdict: for ASCII bodies, which are nearly all logged bodies, the truncated path costs 3.4× the complete one (2.31 vs 0.64 µs at the shipped 16 KiB cap); in system terms 500 truncated bodies/s ≈ 1.2 ms/s of one core, negligible - but the gap is exactly what branch `perf/utf8-truncated-fast-path` closes (a bounded tail check, then the String constructor), so that branch is worth merging on this evidence, with this benchmark re-run on it as the acceptance check.

## 5. Side observations

- The first write run's escape-analysis artifact (section 4 of the metadata block) is a general trap for buffer benchmarks: returning a scalar derived from a non-escaping buffer lets C2 delete the buffer's stores. Return the buffer.
- `FastByteArrayOutputStream.toString(Charset)` and the JDK's `toString(Charset)` are the same operation once the stream holds a single block; the presized Spring stream keeps everything in one block, which is why it matches the JDK stream here. A default-sized Spring stream filled beyond its first block would pay a coalescing copy in `toString` that this benchmark does not exercise.

## 6. Acceptance re-run of the render path after PR #22 (2026-09-17T17:35:57)

- **Commit hash (HEAD at measurement time):** `dbb0a1bb` (full: the merge of PR #23 on top of PR #22, `perf/utf8-truncated-fast-path`); same machine, JVM and JMH settings as above.
- **Raw output:** `./benchmarks/results/body-buffer-render-utf8-fast-path.{txt,json}`; the pre-merge run stays as `./benchmarks/results/body-buffer-render.{txt,json}` for the comparison.
- **What changed under test:** `renderTruncated` now finds the UTF-8 cut with a bounded tail check and renders the prefix through `String(bytes, 0, cut, UTF_8) + note`; every other charset keeps the scratch-buffer loop. The complete path, both streams and the naive rendering are unchanged and serve as the control: they moved by at most 6 % between the runs (the two-byte cases ran 3-6 % faster in the second session across the board - session drift, not a change).

| Body, content | Truncated before | Truncated after | Naive truncated (control) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 KiB ascii | 2.31 µs, 35 176 B | **1.35 µs, 32 920 B** | 1.26 µs, 32 864 B |
| 256 KiB ascii | 37.1 µs, 526 696 B | **23.1 µs, 524 440 B** | 23.6 µs, 524 384 B |
| 16 KiB aä | 11.5 µs, 29 712 B | 8.5 µs, **38 416 B** | 8.7 µs, 38 368 B |
| 256 KiB aä | 182 µs, 439 313 B | 155 µs, **611 865 B** | 167 µs, 611 817 B |

**Verdict - accepted for ASCII, with a named cost for two-byte text.** For ASCII the truncated rendering now equals the naive path in time and bytes (1.35 vs 1.26 µs and 23.1 vs 23.6 µs; 56 B more, the tail check's buffers), which was the target of finding 4.6: at the shipped 16 KiB cap a truncated ASCII body costs 2.2× the complete rendering instead of 3.6×, and 1.6× less than before. For two-byte text the time also drops to the level of the complete rendering (155 vs 151 µs), but the allocation rises by 39 % (439 → 612 KB at 256 KiB, 30 → 38 KB at 16 KiB): the scratch-buffer loop fed the builder straight from the decoder, while the String constructor's Latin1 attempt plus the concatenation copy the prefix twice - the naive path's footprint. That is the trade the fast path makes: nearly all logged bodies are ASCII (JSON, form data, tokens), and for them the gain is a third of the time at equal bytes; a body of mostly non-ASCII text pays 0.7 bytes more allocation per buffered byte on the truncated path only, in the young generation. No further change recommended; the next step would only be worth taking if a deployment logs truncated non-ASCII bodies at a rate where 170 KB of extra transient allocation per body shows up in GC metrics, and it would be a content check before choosing the path, not a return to the loop.
