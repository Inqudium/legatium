## 1. Executive summary

This is the fourth defect analysis of the codebase and the second today. Between the previous report (`DEFECT_ANALYSIS-2026-09-19T17-45-47`, commit `7556a09`) and this commit, 21 of its 22 findings were fixed on two branches - the reactive twin's second-subscription defect, the test gaps around it, and the body-meter cache the two September-16 reports had asked for - and every one of those fixes was re-verified here as present and effective (section 2, "Carried items"). The twins' lifecycle code - bounded body capture, exactly-once completion, fail-open guards, additive MDC/context restoration, twin symmetry - is unchanged and was found sound again.

The new defects sit in the one piece of production code that changed: the body-meter cache in `ClientLoggingMetrics`. **One High:** the cache resolves a meter INSIDE `ConcurrentHashMap.computeIfAbsent`, i.e. while holding the map's bin lock, and that resolution takes Micrometer's registry-wide `meterMapLock`; the removal listener the cache installs runs under that same `meterMapLock` and removes entries from the map, which takes the bin lock. Two threads, two locks, opposite order: a host that removes meters concurrently (Kafka client metrics on a rebalance, a registry `clear()`) while another thread registers a first-time tag set can deadlock the registry and the exchange, on the blocking stack the caller's thread, on the reactive one an event loop. Rare - it needs the coincidence and a shared hash bin - but the lock order is verified in both sources and the impact is an outage. **One Medium:** the cache "mirrors the registry's entries" only for meters the registry accepts; a denying `MeterFilter` (Boot's `management.metrics.enable.adapter.*=false`, a `maximumAllowableTags` cap) makes Micrometer return an unregistered no-op meter that the cache still keeps per tag set, so exactly the operator who disabled the meters to protect the registry from the caller-controlled `host` tag gets an unbounded cache instead. Two Low items round this off: the pre-registered fixed meters are not covered by the removal listener, and the owner's lifetime KDoc is stale.

Everything else is test quality: the reactive twin's `PER_CLASS` fixture fix turned Boot's prototype `WebClient.Builder` into a per-class shared object, and the response-timeout test - which JUnit's default order runs FIRST - leaves a 200 ms connector on it for the six tests after it, so the `cancelled`-vs-`timeout` proof now races two equal timers on every run (Medium); the shared buffer's fuzz target runs against the empty input only and is absent from the nightly matrix (Medium); an `exclude-path-prefixes`-only activation is proven nowhere (Medium); and a tail of hygiene items (a vacuous gzip assertion, one static peer slot, one unclosed client, unbounded `block()`s, two imprecise fuzz oracles, a non-hermetic environment, a few unpinned guards).

**Test verdict.** *Reliability as a safety net:* high across all three modules - the score-5 suites go red under the mutation experiments applied here (the `Int` doubling, a skipped MDC rollback, a dropped removal listener, a non-atomic counter, a case-sensitive exclude, the `subscribed` flag removed, the `freeze()` calls removed, a swapped `304` rule), fault injection is real (a globally installed failing MDC adapter, throwing counters and summaries, a decoder refusing progress, a body that refuses a second subscription), and the risky logic is checked deterministically with an injected clock and pinned ids. *Usage & coverage:* the pyramid is base-heavy - roughly 420 unit and contract tests carry the behaviour; nine real-engine suites (five request factories, four connectors, now including the `exchangeToMono` scenario that caught the last High), Boot-builder and Brave tracing integrations and the consumer smoke test verify the seams; three of four fuzz targets run with corpora. *Most significant gaps and anomalies:*

- The reactive twin's Boot integration suite is contaminated by its own first test: a shared prototype builder carries a 200 ms response timeout into every later test, and the `cancelled` proof can flip to `timeout` under load (finding 3).
- `BoundedByteBufferFuzzTest` is dead weight in both modes - no seed corpus, not in the `Fuzz` workflow's matrix - while its Javadoc claims otherwise (finding 4).
- No test in the repository configures `exclude-path-prefixes` without `include-path-patterns`; the "nothing configured" short-circuit in `ClientActivation` can be inverted without a red test (finding 5).
- The new cache's own edges are unpinned: key discrimination by template and host, and the guard on the read-state counter (findings 8, 9).
- The reactive twin's integration awaits remain blind to a surplus event (pattern P3, carried and open by decision).

## 2. Scope & methodology

**Modules and roles.** `./legatium-common/` holds the byte-identical shared code the two twins inline through Shade (ADR-0003): the bounded buffer, properties and their origins, header selection and masking, activation, identity (`traceparent`, correlation header, counting id), classification, the metrics owner (now with the body-meter cache), MDC scope, timeout classification and the observation-wiring report. `./legatium-restclient-logging/` is the blocking twin (a `ClientHttpRequestInterceptor` whose emission point is `ClientHttpResponse.close()`), `./legatium-webclient-logging/` the reactive twin (an `ExchangeFilterFunction` with two hand-written Reactor operators whose emission point is the body publisher's terminal signal). `./consumer-smoke/` checks the shaded jars as a consumer sees them; `./benchmarks/` holds JMH measurements of the buffer and, since this round, of the body-meter cache.

**Stack detected.** Spring Boot 4.1.1 on Spring Framework 7.0.9, Micrometer 1.17.1; both blocking (`RestClient`/`RestTemplate`) and reactive (`WebClient`, Reactor) client stacks; no coroutines; virtual threads neither configured nor assumed; no persistence, messaging, scheduling or controllers - this is a library, whose entry points are the interceptor, the filter, two auto-configurations, three customizers and one `SmartInitializingSingleton` per twin. Optional dependencies: `spring-boot-restclient`/`spring-boot-webclient` (customizers), `io.micrometer:context-propagation` (ADR-0010 restoration).

**Test code in scope: yes.** The three modules carry 139, 144 and 139 declared test methods (plus 3 in `consumer-smoke`), counted by annotation; the blocking contract's 6 methods run in five engine suites and the reactive contract's 5 in four, so 168 resp. 154 executions. Test-quality heuristics were applied to every non-trivial test (evidential value by mutation thought experiment against the production code, determinism, isolation, risk-path coverage, sham concurrency, resource lifecycle, convention deviations against `./CONTRIBUTING.md`). The test review was split by module across three parallel reviewers working from the same production-code reading; every finding they reported was re-verified by the author against the current code before inclusion (the fixture, the builder's bean scope, the method order, the fuzz matrix, the activation tests, the fuzz oracle), and their rejected candidates are listed in section 5 so nobody re-investigates them.

**Procedure.** Phase 1 ranked every production and test file (section 4). Phase 2 read every production file completely, concentrating on the code that changed since the previous report - the body-meter cache and its removal listener, the `subscribed` flag of `ObservedBody`, the new `BodyMeterRecordBenchmark` - and re-read the twins' lifecycle for regressions the fixes could have introduced; the cache was traced into Micrometer's `getOrCreateMeter`/`remove` and the JDK's `ConcurrentHashMap.computeIfAbsent`/`removeValueIf` line by line, both from the versions the build resolves. Phase 3 checked each candidate against reachability, framework contracts, ADRs, the module guide and the existing tests; style and taste items were discarded unless a documented convention was violated.

**Carried items, re-verified.** Of the previous report's 22 findings, 1-18 and 20-22 were verified as fixed and effective at this commit: the `subscribed` flag (`ObservedBody.kt:79-88`) with the single-subscriber unit test, the unit-level `exchangeToMono` test and the connector-contract scenario on all four connectors; the freeze pin (red with the two `freeze()` lines removed); the armed failing-adapter suite of the blocking twin; the 304/1xx cases; the delta assertion; the `closing(...)` registration; the bounded `get`; the `PER_CLASS` fixture (which introduced finding 3 here); the scope-throws-on-close tests in both twins; the handler-invocation count; the exact-suffix oracle; the awaited worker futures; the `Content-Type: garbage` tests; the failed zero-copy pin; the Dependabot directory; the cache with its removal test (red with the listener deleted); the two fixture rows; the seven-row identity table. Finding 19 is still open by its recorded decision (finding 20 here). Pattern P1 (meter re-resolution) is closed by the cache; P2 (fail-open guards) has two residual sites (pattern P4 here); P3 (awaits blind to surplus) is unchanged.

**No local verification took place**: no build, no test run, no benchmark. Finding 1 rests on the two lock orders read in the framework and JDK sources and on a documented `ConcurrentHashMap` contract, not on a reproduced deadlock; finding 3's order dependence rests on a recomputation of JUnit's default sorter, not on an observed run.

**Known blind spots.** Connector and engine behaviour outside the nine engine families the tests cover; third-party `ClientResponse`/`ClientHttpResponse`/`MeterRegistry` implementations that violate their contracts; performance magnitudes beyond the checked-in JMH results; pure security, release infrastructure and dependency advisories are outside this report.
